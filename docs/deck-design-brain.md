# Deck design brain

How to decide what a slide should look like and how to build it. This is the *reasoning* layer for presentation decks; the hard layout rules (fixed 1280×720 canvas, 48px padding, grid, title scale) live in `CLAUDE.md` → "Presentation decks", and the mechanics of `.deck-slide` live in `base/deck-layout.css`. Read those first; this doc tells you how to *think*.

The core principle (from CLAUDE.md): **compose every slide from design tokens and atomic components upward, and be creative with anything larger.** There are no deck-specific components. Every recipe below is a *composition* of existing atomic/page-level pieces and raw tokens — reshape them freely (horizontal cards, mixed sub-components, side-by-side or stacked), never treat a web component as a fixed unit.

**When a reshape recurs**, promote it to a documented component variant (in the component's own CSS) rather than re-declaring the same override in slide after slide — e.g. a horizontal card layout, or a brand-coloured stat figure. Slide-local CSS is fine for a genuine one-off; it is a smell once you've written it twice.

This is a living document. New reference slides refine it — see "E. Extending the brain" at the end.

---

## A. Design DNA — the invariants

These are what make a slide read as Smartcat. Hold them constant no matter what you're building. Values are the source-of-truth tokens; the hex shown is for recognition only.

- **Type.** Headings in Plus Jakarta Sans Bold (`--family-heading`), body in Inter Regular (`--family-body`). Headlines are large and tight, and frequently **end with a period** as a stylistic device — "Deck heading.", "Thank you.". Match that voice. Those two families are the whole set — never a third, and no italic serif, monospace chip or other guest face dropped into a headline. Tracking is never hand-set — every size ships with its own `--letter-spacing-*` token, and there is no per-theme weight adjustment on a dark slide either. **Body copy is set at 1.15 leading and never at "single"**: paragraphs, card copy, captions, bullets and table cells all take it. A slide is read at a distance, and single-spaced 18px body sets far too tight for that; headings and display figures keep their own tight default.
- **Micro type has one job and one place.** Sources, footnotes and disclaimers are caption scale in `--content-static-tertiary`, parked in the same position on every slide that carries them (normally bottom-left, under the content). Its smallness is the point — never promote it to body size to fill space, and never shrink it below the caption token to fit.
- **Title placement.** The **cover** anchors its headline left-aligned in the **lower-middle band** of the canvas — clearly below center, but with visible room left below it for a subtitle or closing metadata, never flush at the very bottom edge (see the Cover recipe in section C for why the exact position varies). **Every other slide** anchors its title **top-left**, flush at the 48px padding origin.
- **Whitespace is deliberate.** Most content slides fill only ~60–70% of the canvas. The title sits alone at the top with a large gap (the fixed 80px heading→content gap) before content, and content rarely runs to the bottom edge. Do not fill empty space just because it's there — restraint is the look.
- **A slide needs enough content to earn the canvas.** This is different from "whitespace is deliberate" above, which is about restraint in an otherwise-complete slide. This is about a slide that never had enough to begin with: a bare title + one short sentence, or any single skinny element with the bottom half of the slide empty, isn't a slide — it's a fragment. Before treating something as its own slide, check whether it actually fills a meaningful share of the canvas at a normal information density. If it doesn't, fold it into a neighboring slide under one shared idea instead — a one-line claim becomes the subtitle under a heading+paragraph, or a lone stat becomes the third item in a numbers row.
- **Layout is not always even, and not always one layer.** An even `.grid` row of equal-width panels and a single heading+paragraph are two of the recipes below — correct and common, not the only shapes. Reference decks also split a slide at an *uneven* ratio (a short highlighted stat beside a wider table; a narrow narrative beside a bigger card grid) and *stack* two different components vertically in one slide (a process chain with a supporting card row underneath it; a quote panel over its attribution bar). See "Asymmetric & stacked layouts" in section C, and the decision procedure in section D for when each earns its place over the even/single-layer default.
- **Color.**
  - Neutrals: near-black `#13101C` text / off-white + gray surfaces in light mode; white text / near-black surfaces in dark mode. Always via `--content-*` and `--background-static-gray-layer-*` tokens.
  - **Brand gradient** (the signature accent): white → pink `--color-non-semantic-pink-70` (`#c326ed`) → purple `#731EF2` → indigo, left-to-right, always as a **flat linear fill with a sharp edge**. Used for hero moments — section-divider backgrounds, the testimonial/comparison "hero" panel, chart fills. Not for ordinary content, and **never a blurred radial blob, orb, or glow** — that generic "AI-marketing" mesh-gradient look is explicitly banned (see CLAUDE.md → "Section backgrounds beyond the gray layers"). A decorative accent, if one is wanted, is a flat brand-purple shape with a hard edge — not a soft gradient glow. (The hex here was corrected 2026-09-18 — `#DA00FE` doesn't exist in `tokens/globals.css`'s pink ramp; pink-70 is the actual token this was describing, confirmed by pixel-sampling the cover gradient below, which uses the same pink family.)
  - **Cover gradient** (cover-only, distinct from the brand gradient above): a **vertical** ramp — flat dark (`--background-static-gray-layer-0`, dark mode, ≈`#1a1824`) for the top ~45–50% of the canvas, then a smooth transition down through brand purple (`#731ef2`, ~75–85% down) into pink (`--color-non-semantic-pink-70`/`60`, `#c326ed`–`#cf48f4`) at the very bottom edge. Confirmed by pixel-sampling three real cover slides (`references/decks/cover/`) — all three use the identical ramp. This is the **only** place this specific vertical dark→purple→pink treatment appears; do not reuse it as a general "dark gradient" for ordinary content slides (those use the horizontal brand gradient above, or a solid fill), and never substitute a soft radial blob for it.
  - **Brand purple** `#731EF2` for single-color brand emphasis — stat figures, chart primaries, active/"our" elements, number badges. For text and figures the token is **`--content-static-brand`** (not a background token) — and it is **theme-aware**: purple-70 in light mode, **purple-50 in dark**. Purple-70 on a near-black slide is too dark to read, which is exactly why the token carries a dark-mode value; a brand-coloured *fill* (a numbered badge, a pill) keeps purple-70 in both themes, since it carries white on top.
  - **Semantic green/red** (`--...-positive` / `--...-negative`) **only for genuinely positive/negative meaning** — highlights vs lowlights, Gantt done vs pending, thumbs up/down. Never decoratively.
  - **One accent job per slide.** The brand accent does exactly *one* of these at a time: the key figure *or* one accent word in the headline — never both. Never a label above the title: a line set above a heading is an eyebrow whatever colour or casing it carries, and eyebrows are banned system-wide (CLAUDE.md → "Text casing"). Some reference slides (`references/decks/dark/exec-summary-3col.jpg`, `workflow-steps.jpg`) do carry one — they predate the ban; do not copy it. Same logic one level up: **one chromatic idea per slide**. If the layout is busy the type stays plain; if the type is the artwork the ground stays flat.
  - **Accent word in a headline.** Setting one word or short phrase of a headline in `--content-static-brand` is sanctioned — it is the deck's instance of the same device as a document cover's `.doc-hero__title-accent` and a social canvas's `.social-canvas__accent`. It is a *title* treatment, not prose emphasis, so the no-inline-bold rule doesn't reach it. One per slide, headline only, never inside body copy.
  - **Tonal ramp.** A run of surfaces stepping through `--background-static-brand-layer-1` → `-2` → `-3` → `-inverted` may encode **real rank or escalation** across a card set or stat ladder (low→high, stage 1→4). Only when the ordering is genuine — used decoratively it is just four different purples. The ramp inverts in dark mode (the layer tokens flip), which is correct: it stays monotone either way.
- **Emphasis ladder — in this order.** Size, then weight, then tone (drop the supporting half to `--content-static-secondary`), then the one accent word or figure. That is the whole vocabulary. Two token-only devices are worth naming because they buy hierarchy with no size change: the **two-tone title** (line 1 in `--content-static-primary`, line 2 in `--content-static-secondary`, same size and weight) and the **two-tone paragraph** (lead sentence primary, the rest secondary, one size). Both split at a whole-line or whole-sentence boundary — never mid-sentence, which would just be inline emphasis. Not in the vocabulary: underline (it means "link" in this system), outline or ghost type, marker-pen highlights, and inline bold inside a sentence (CLAUDE.md → "Text casing").
- **Surfaces (panels/cards).** Rounded corners, generous internal padding. On **light** backgrounds → solid gray layer-1 panels. On **dark** backgrounds → the frosted alpha panel in CSS; in pptx, which has no backdrop blur, its flat stand-in is **gray-80 (`#1F1D2A`)**, one step off the slide's own gray-90 ground. Any lighter and the panel reads as a grey box floating on the slide instead of a surface sunk into it. One featured panel per comparison may use the brand gradient fill. **Every content card carries a visual anchor** — a design-system icon or a numbered circle badge — never heading+paragraph text alone. The one exception is a stat card (Numbers/stats recipe): the large figure itself is the anchor, so no icon or number is needed there. **A narrow card takes a smaller heading.** Below roughly 260px — a card panel inside a split, a 4-up row — the H3 heading wraps a three-word phrase onto three lines and swamps the copy beneath it; step it down to H4. The same applies to a **stat figure on a narrow card**: a Display figure needs about 170px for something like "400%", so below that width it steps down to H1 rather than wrapping its last glyph out of the card. It is still the largest thing on the card, so it still reads as the anchor. **Size a card to its own content, not to the slide's full available height** — a card with one short sentence should be visibly shorter than one with two. When a row of shorter cards leaves the slide with extra vertical room below the card row, center the row in the space between the heading gap and the bottom padding rather than stretching cards to fill it or pinning them to the top with dead space underneath. A card's anchor may also be a **check badge** (a filled circle holding the design system's check icon) when the row is a confirmation list — same slot as an icon or number badge. **The two anchor kinds are not the same size or colour.** A numbered badge is the smaller disc, solid brand purple with a white numeral, in both themes. An **icon** anchor is a visibly larger disc, and it reads against the *surface* it sits on rather than the theme: on any card whose fill is not pure white — which is every card in both themes — the disc is **white** with a brand-purple glyph, falling back to the brand tint only on a genuinely white surface, where a white disc would vanish. Both kinds take the same generous gap down to the heading; a badge crowding its own text is the single most common way a card row looks unconsidered. When a card set is deliberately uneven — a bento of several small cards and one hero card anchoring the grid — **vary the card size, never the radius or the gap**: one radius token and one gutter value across the whole grid is what keeps it reading as a single object. A row of images obeys the same law: **same aspect ratio, same radius, same treatment on every crop** — equal crops are what make twelve mismatched headshots or logos read as one set.
- **A missing image or logo asset gets a placeholder, not a gap.** Where a real asset belongs — a customer logo, a partner/tool icon tile, a product screenshot — and isn't available at build time, render a visible placeholder instead of omitting the visual or leaving bare text: a dashed-border panel (gray layer-1 fill, no image) holding the design system's `placeholder` icon centered, with a small caption naming what belongs there (e.g. "Logo: Adobe Experience Manager", "Screenshot: workspace setup"). This keeps the slide's intended composition legible and gives whoever finishes the deck an exact, labeled slot to drop the real asset into — never fewer elements than the layout calls for.
- **Imagery.** Slides draw on the same sanctioned visual types as the rest of the system (CLAUDE.md → "Visual type per media slot") — product-UI screenshot, conceptual illustration, icon tile, design-system vector icon, monochrome customer logo, circle-crop portrait. **Never generic stock photography**, and never a treatment the system doesn't have (duotone, halftone, macro texture, abstract 3D); pick one treatment and hold it across the whole deck. **Screenshots**: rounded corners, no border and no shadow, cropped to the part that proves the point — the hairline-bordered, soft-shadowed frame is a *document*-tier rule, not a deck one (see "No drop shadows" above). **Type over an image**, in escalating order: pick or crop to an image with a quiet zone → set the type on its own surface over the image (the frosted alpha panel from Surfaces above) → split the canvas and stop fighting it. **Never fix legibility on the type** — no drop shadows, outlines or strokes; fix the ground. One cell of an otherwise-regular grid may be an image instead of a card — that is the "hierarchy within a repeated component" device below, used deliberately and once.
- **A slide about a product surface reserves room for it.** When a slide's claim is about something the product actually *looks* like — the conversation you drop a file into, the side-by-side review view, a dashboard — the layout holds a **reserved image slot** for a screenshot inserted by hand later (`draw_image_slot`). Three things follow from that:
  - **This is not the missing-asset placeholder.** That one (`add_placeholder`, above) stands in for an asset we meant to have and didn't. A reserved slot is a composition decision made before anyone looks for an image — **not having a real screenshot at build time is the normal case for this mechanism, never a reason to withhold it.** The deck's job is to reserve the space and describe the shot; producing the actual image is a separate, later step for someone else. They are drawn differently on purpose — the reserved slot is brand-purple and states its exact pixel size and aspect ratio, because someone has to go and produce an image of precisely that shape. **Never label a slot with a ratio it isn't**: a banner marked "16:9" that is really 3.95:1 sends the exporter after the wrong crop, which is the one failure the label exists to prevent.
  - **Only where a specific feature is named.** "See before and after, side by side" earns a slot. "SOC 2 compliant" does not, and neither does a benefits row or a list of file formats. A screenshot on every slide is the generic SaaS look, and it is worse than none.
  - **Screenshots are inserted, never drawn.** Decks embed the real image, the way documents do — do **not** reconstruct product UI from atomics. That is the *social* tier's rule, for a thumbnail-sized illustration of the product; a deck shows the product.
- **Three slot shapes, all taken from the reference decks** (`Deck refs/Image placement`):
  - **Side panel** — the slot fills one side of an asymmetric split, copy on the other. The commonest shape by far, and the default. It holds a 3:2 landscape proportion rather than stretching to whatever the facing panel needs.
  - **Banner** — the slot runs the full content width under the title, for a slide where the screenshot *is* the argument and the copy is one supporting line (`add_image_banner`).
  - **Grid cell** — one or more cells of a card row given over to imagery, at exactly the card's own size, radius and gutter, so the row still reads as one object. Pass `image` instead of `heading` on that card.
- **Add a slot freely, whether or not a screenshot exists yet — the obligation is to report it, not to avoid it.** Lacking the real asset at build time is the ordinary case for a reserved slot, not an exception to the rule; skipping the slot because the screenshot isn't ready yet defeats the entire mechanism. What must never happen is *silent* delivery: `image_slots(prs)` returns every reserved slot with its slide number and caption — hand that over as a shot list on every handoff, and say plainly the deck isn't finished until they're filled. A reported slot is normal; an unreported one is the actual failure.
- **Gutter vs. slide padding.** The 8px gutter (`--grid-gutter`) is for the gap between two adjacent elements that **each carry their own visible surface** — stacked cards, side-by-side panels — where the tight gap reads as edges within one grid. It assumes a surface on both sides of the gap. When one side of a gap is **bare** (no background of its own — a hero stat, a bare heading, plain text) next to a paneled neighbour, there is no second edge for the gutter to relate to; use the slide's own padding value instead (**48px**, `--spacing-9`), so the bare content reads as sitting in the slide's whitespace rather than glued to the panel. Apply this per adjacency, not globally — override that one row's gap locally; do not redefine `--grid-gutter` itself, since other rows/stacks in the same slide may still be panel-to-panel and need the true 8px gutter.
- **No drop shadows — ever.** Not on cards, panels, icon tiles, arrows, tables or images. A surface is separated from its ground by fill and radius alone, which is how every reference slide does it. This needs saying because PowerPoint applies its theme's default shape effects to anything that does not explicitly opt out — the flow-chain arrows shipped with a shadow nobody authored. `deck_pptx.py` now clears effects on every shape it creates and exposes `strip_shadows(prs)` as a final sweep. (Scope: decks. The document tier's `.doc-screenshot` frame and the onepager tier's card shadows are unchanged — they are printed formats with their own rules.)
- **No chrome — ever.** Smartcat slides carry no running head, no page number, no per-slide logo, no section rail, tab strip or progress indicator. Confirmed across every reference slide in `references/decks/`: the wordmark appears on the cover, the monogram on a comparison's "ours" panel, and nowhere else. Navigation is carried entirely by the section dividers and the light/dark banding (section B) — do not invent a corner kit, a footer rule or a "you are here" marker. Any interactive version of one (jump-to pills, a "back to contents" link) is doubly out, per the next bullet.
- **No interactive UI — ever.** A deck is presented and viewed, not operated. The button paradigm (`.btn`, hover/active states, a click affordance) belongs to web UI and has no place on a slide. For a call to action, next step, or contact info, set it as **plain or link-styled text** (`--content-link-default` for a hyperlink-like emphasis, or just regular text) — never a pill, fill, border, or anything shaped like a clickable button. This applies everywhere a web instinct might reach for `.btn`, most commonly the closing slide.

## B. Choose the background and theme

Theme and background are **two independent, composable axes** (set on `.deck-slide`: `data-theme` × `data-layer`). Decide both per slide, then keep them steady across a run of related slides.

**Theme** — light is the default; dark is for rhythm and emphasis. Alternate in *bands* (a run of related slides), not per slide, so the deck scans as chapters. Section dividers are a natural place to flip.

**Background** — pick the least intense one that does the job:

| Background | `.deck-slide` | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Solid white | light, layer-0 | The majority of regular light slides. |
| Solid gray | light, layer-1 | Light slides that need to differ from their neighbours; light-mode section titles. |
| Dark gradient (flat brand-gradient wash, sharp-edged) | dark | Regular dark slides; can also carry a light-mode section title. |
| Solid black | dark, layer-0 | Regular dark slides; strong section titles. |
| **Brand / inverted background** | `data-layer="brand"` (no theme flip — see note) | Hero moments only: section dividers, the closing "thank you", at most 1–2 per deck. |

Rule of thumb: **content slides** lean solid (white / gray / black); **gradients are reserved for dividers and the open/close** — and even there, a gradient is always a flat wash, never a soft blob/orb/glow (see Design DNA → Color). There is no "mesh gradient" asset in this system — do not improvise one with a CSS radial gradient or a blurred shape; that is exactly the banned pattern.

**Note on brand/inverted backgrounds.** `background-static-brand-inverted` (and every other `*-inverted` background token) is a self-contained pair with `--content-static-inverted` — it already contrasts correctly in whichever theme is ambient (dark purple + white text in light mode; pale purple + near-black text in dark mode). Do **not** add `data-theme="dark"` to "get" white text — that flips the background to the pale dark-mode value while independently flipping content to white, which is illegible. `base/deck-layout.css` handles this correctly by repointing `--content-static-primary`/`secondary` to `--content-static-inverted` on `data-layer="brand"`; when hand-building a slide outside that mechanism (e.g. authoring raw shapes), use the inverted background with the inverted content color directly, in one theme, with no flip.

## C. Slide-role catalog — recipes

Each role: *when to reach for it*, and *how to compose it* from tokens + atomic/existing pieces. These are starting points — combine and reshape as the content demands.

### Openers & dividers
- **Cover.** Grounded in three real reference covers (`references/decks/cover/`) — consistent across all three:
  - **Background: always the cover gradient** (Design DNA → Color), never solid or the horizontal brand gradient. Dark theme throughout, white text.
  - **Logo lockup, top-left, always present.** The Smartcat wordmark (white) alone for a generic, non-client asset (an ungated content piece, a template). When the deck is for a specific named account, pair it with that account's own logo beside it, small gap, same baseline — never Smartcat alone once a client is named, and never a decorative element added to either mark (see CLAUDE.md → "Logo").
  - **Title: large, bold, white, left-aligned, 2–3 lines with deliberately chosen line breaks** (not left to auto-wrap) — sits in the lower-middle band per Design DNA "Title placement," roughly where the gradient is transitioning from dark to purple. Its exact vertical position isn't fixed — it sits higher when a metadata block below needs room (see next), lower when the cover is otherwise bare.
  - **Optional subtitle directly under the title** — one short sentence framing the deck's purpose ("A guide for enterprise teams turning…", "A business case for governed localization…"), lighter weight than the title, in **purple-40 (`#C5B3FC`)**. Not white: the cover's two small lines are tinted, not neutral.
  - **Optional metadata block — pick at most one, or omit it entirely; never combine two:**
    - *Top-right proposal info* (formal external proposal): a thin horizontal divider rule, a short framing line ("A strategic proposal for [Client]"), another divider, then "Prepared for: [name, title]" — small, muted, right-aligned.
    - *Bottom two-column prepared-by/for* (account-team business case): near the bottom edge, two columns, each a thin divider rule over a small label ("Prepared by" / "Prepared for") with names below (a bold team/lead line, then individual names).
    - *Bottom footer line* (lightweight): copyright + confidentiality + doc type + date on one small line, separated by middle-dots — e.g. "© 2026 Smartcat, Inc. — Confidential · Strategic partnership review · June 2026." Sentence case throughout: a reference sample had its doc-type label in all-caps, which this system's text-casing rule overrides — never copy that verbatim.
    - *None* — the simplest generic cover is just logo + title + optional subtitle, nothing else.
    Whichever metadata block is used, its small type is **pink-40 (`#EDB7FB`)** — a step warmer than the purple-40 subtitle, so the cover's two quiet lines stay distinguishable from each other against the gradient.
  - No eyebrow label above the title (confirmed absent in all three references, consistent with the system-wide ban); no interactive UI.
  - Build from `heading` (title + optional subtitle) plus the logo and whichever metadata block fits; no other decorative object — restraint is still the look here, the gradient and logo lockup carry the visual interest.
- **Section divider.** One big title **top-left** (Display scale), full-bleed background, no body. This is where you flip theme/background for chapter rhythm — solid gray, dark, or brand gradient. Build from `heading` alone.
- **One big statement.** A single sentence at large scale, vertically centered or top-left, nothing else. Build from `heading` (title-only) or `text-block`.
- **Opening hook — the slide after the cover.** Open on a reason to care, not on the agenda: a change in the world, a shared frustration, an unexpected number, or a question. Any role below can carry it — most often one big statement, a heading + paragraph, or a single stat. When a deck genuinely needs an agenda slide, it follows the hook rather than replacing it.
- **At-a-glance summary.** Everything the deck concludes, on one slide, before the chapters start — one numbered card per finding (the numbered-badge anchor from Design DNA), a short claim each. Use it on any deck long enough to have chapters; in a read deck (see section D) treat it as the slide most people will actually read. Grounded in `references/decks/dark/exec-summary-3col.jpg`, which pairs it with a summary paragraph panel in an asymmetric split.

### Text-forward
- **Heading + paragraph.** Title top-left, one paragraph below after the 80px gap, occupying the left ~half. Optional subtitle line under the title (muted, `--content-static-secondary`). Build from `heading` (`data-paragraph`). The paragraph needs to be a real paragraph, not a single clause padding out an otherwise-empty slide — see Design DNA, "A slide needs enough content to earn the canvas." A short supporting bullet list may sit under or beside it (see next).
- **Bulleted list — a supporting element only, never the whole slide.** A column of bullets is a fine way to add a short supporting list next to a paragraph, inside a split panel, or under a heading. But a genuinely enumerable, parallel set of items (3 or more real peers) is a cards job, not a list job, even when each item is short — see "Enumerated content" below and decision-procedure step 3.
- **N columns of text.** Title + 2–3 equal text columns (each an optional sub-heading + paragraph) across a `.grid` row. Use for parallel explanations with no visual per item.

### Asymmetric & stacked layouts
These aren't a separate "purpose" the way the sections around them are — they're a reshape that applies across several purposes above and below. Reach for them when the content itself is uneven or layered; don't apply them for variety's sake to content that's genuinely parallel (that's still N cards).
- **Asymmetric split.** Two panels at an uneven width ratio (e.g. 5:7 or 4:8), not an even column division — a short narrative beside a wider card grid, or one highlighted stat beside a comparison table. Use when one side is inherently smaller (a framing sentence, a single headline figure) and the other carries the supporting detail; forcing both to the same width under-serves whichever side actually has less to say. Panel gap follows the gutter-vs-padding rule above: 8px when both sides carry their own surface, **64px** (`--spacing-11`) when one side is bare text or a bare heading — a bare column needs more air against a paneled neighbour than two panels need against each other. **Both panels share one top edge, and the pair is centered as a single block** in the space below the title. Letting each side decide its own vertical position is how a split ends up with its text pinned under the title and its cards floating in the middle.
- **Stacked composition.** Two different components stacked vertically in one slide instead of side by side — a process chain with a supporting detail-card row underneath, or a quote panel over an attribution bar (the existing Testimonial recipe below is one instance of this, not a one-off). Use when a slide needs both a headline element and the detail behind it, and putting them side by side would starve one of them of width.
- **Hierarchy within a repeated component.** A row of otherwise-identical cards need not stay uniform — one card can be visually promoted (a brand-purple fill or border, a small "recommended"/featured marker) to read as the featured option among peers, without breaking out of the N-cards shape entirely.

### Enumerated content
- **N cards (2–4, or a wider grid).** Title + a `.grid` row of panels, each = a visual anchor (icon or numbered circle badge — see Design DNA) + sub-heading + paragraph. Light → gray panels; dark → frosted alpha panels. **This is also the default recipe for any genuinely enumerable, parallel list** — a plain bullet list of several peer items reads flat; give each item a card instead. Reshape to horizontal (media left, text right) when useful. One card in the row may be promoted over its neighbours — see "Hierarchy within a repeated component" above — when the content has a genuine featured/recommended option; don't promote a card just to break up the row. Card height follows its own content, not the slide's full available height — see Design DNA, "Surfaces."
- **Icon cards.** The same recipe, called out separately only because the icon is the point — a distinct, meaningful icon per item rather than a generic anchor. Leads with a circular icon tile (design-system icon, brand-purple) above a caption + paragraph. Built from `cards` (`data-media="icon"`).
- **Grouped tiles.** A categorized enumeration — several sub-groups, each with its own short list of items (e.g. tools grouped by category) — is still a cards job at heart, just one level deeper: render each group as its own labeled cluster of small tiles, one tile per item, optionally with a logo (or a placeholder — see Design DNA) rather than a nested bullet list. Reach for this instead of "heading + paragraph with a bulleted sub-list" whenever the supporting content is itself enumerable.
- **Agenda.** Full-width stacked rows (~65% width), each = an icon tile on the left + a label. Emphasize the final/action row with a brand-purple fill. Compose from atomic icon tiles + token-styled rows.
- **Row-per-item.** Many short updates, each a claim plus a detail: one full-width row per item, the claim as a real sub-heading element (H4/H5 scale, `--content-static-primary`) over or beside the detail in `--content-static-secondary`. The tone split is what makes it skimmable — never produce that effect with inline bold inside a sentence. This is the densest honest way to fit 5–8 items that each need a sentence; past that, split the slide.
- **Pills.** The `badge`/pill atomic is the right element for tags, stage labels, status markers and the compact process strip described under Flow/graph — short standalone labels. It is not a container for content: a node that has to carry a caption, or an item that has to carry a paragraph, needs a real surface, not a widened pill.
- **Where the label goes.** Above the row when it labels a **comparison set** (the reader needs it before reading across); below when it is a **caption** describing what they just looked at. Pick one per slide and hold it.

### Quantitative & proof
- **Numbers / stats.** Title + a `.grid` row of 2–4 panels, each = a big **brand-purple** figure (Display scale) + bold caption + supporting paragraph. Built from `numbers`. **This is the one card-like recipe exempt from the icon/number-badge anchor rule** — the stat figure itself is the anchor. It is *not* exempt from the height rule, though: a stat card still sizes to its own content and centers in its row rather than stretching — see Design DNA "Surfaces." **A stat card carrying only a figure and a caption — no description line — takes a deeper bottom padding** than an even inset would give it: a short caption sitting directly under a Display figure otherwise looks dropped against the card's floor. Note: `numbers.css` paints `.numbers__value` in `--content-static-primary`; for the deck's brand-purple figure set the value colour to `--content-static-brand`. That override recurs — a candidate to graduate into a `numbers` brand-figure variant. **Four parts make a statistic**: the number, what it means, optionally progress against a target, and its **source** — a caption-scale line in `--content-static-tertiary`. Drop the source and the number stops being evidence; credit everything the same way (data sources, methodology notes, photo credits). **Round aggressively** — "$266K", not "$266,006.40"; precision belongs in an appendix. A **comparison figure** (last year's number, the market baseline) may sit beside the live one at the same type size in `--content-static-tertiary` — two tiers of metric, one size, no second accent colour; it is the stat-card form of the purple-vs-neutral series rule under Charts. Numbers can also be the *whole* slide: a hairline-ruled grid of figures with their captions, no panels and no chart furniture — a legitimate alternative to a row of stat cards once there are more than four.
- **Charts.** Title + a `.grid` row of panels, each holding one simple chart (donut, bar pair, arrow/pentagon). **Purple = the primary/highlighted series; white/outline = the comparison series.** Caption paragraph under each. Compose chart shapes from token-colored elements. Four rules carry more than the chart type does: **headline the finding, not the data** (the title says what to conclude — "Rework costs two weeks per launch" — while the axis says what is plotted); **annotate the marks and kill the legend** (series labelled on the mark, value at the end of the bar, gridline values inside the plot, categories ordered by value — the only honest legend exceptions are repeating stacked bars and a 2–4 slice pie with its values inside the slices); **one accent series, everything else neutral** (the purple/outline rule above); and **put the value on the object** — if the reader has to look away from a mark to learn what it means, the chart failed.

  Chart forms built from rectangles, circles and text, and therefore on-token:
  - **Bar-in-track** — equal-length pale tracks, a filled brand-purple portion, the value in inverted text inside the fill. The best pattern for ranked percentages.
  - **Proportional colour blocks** — columns whose widths encode the split, label on top, the percentage large at the bottom.
  - **Stat bands** — full-content-width horizontal bands, figure left, caption right, one band per metric; one band may be an image instead (see Imagery).
  - **Drill-down bar** — a bar plus a second bar breaking out one of its segments, joined by dashed leaders, for "of that slice, most is X".
  - **Unit chart** — one square or icon per unit with a partial unit for the remainder; only under ~30 units.
  - **Proportional circles** with a right-aligned value ladder, instead of nested TAM/SAM/SOM boxes.
  - **Labelled scatter** — dots coloured by category, label beside each dot, for many items across two dimensions.

  Portability note: several of these (anything with curves or diagonal leaders) don't reduce to native Slides shapes — see `skills/smartcat-deck/SKILL.md`. Flag them rather than approximating them with the wrong shape.
- **Key metrics / OKR.** A row of "KR" cards (white number badge + text) above a "Steps established" sub-heading and a row of numbered step cards. Step number badges may use semantic green. Compose from panels + badges.
- **Highlights vs lowlights.** Two panels side by side, each = heading + a semantic icon badge top-right (green thumbs-up / red thumbs-down) + bulleted list. Compose from panels + semantic-colored atomic badges.
- **Testimonial.** A brand-gradient quote panel (large white quote) stacked over a gray attribution bar: circular photo + name/title on the left, customer logo on the right. Built from `testimonial`, reshaped to the stacked two-part card. Two different situations pull this into an **asymmetric split with a stats panel** instead of standing alone — both produce the same `add_split` shape, and both are still one idea per slide, not an exception carved out of that rule:
  - **The quote's own wording carries the number.** When the quote's wording carries distinct, quantifiable metrics that anchor its credibility (a number that *is* the value proposition — "increased productivity by 50%" — not just narrative color), pull those numbers out into their own stat card(s) beside the quote: quote text on one side (bold pull-quote styling + attribution, no gradient panel needed), 1–2 stat cards on the other, each repeating one number the quote already named.
  - **An aggregate proof slide and an unrelated customer quote proving the same claim.** A stats row drawn from several customers ("50% more productivity," "400% faster turnaround," "70% lower costs") and a quote from a different customer are one idea, not two, when they both support the same underlying claim — typically some form of "customers get the results they came to Smartcat for." The numbers are the quantitative half of that idea and the quote is the qualitative half; merging them is one idea per slide correctly applied, not a workaround around it. This is the general case behind decision-procedure step 1's merge rule below, not a special case of the bullet above: **the quote does not need to repeat the stats' own numbers, only support the same claim they do.**
  Keep the quote as plain, standalone prose (the original stacked-panel form above) when neither applies: a genuinely dedicated customer spotlight (a named account's own case study, given its own slide deliberately for visual weight), a quote that is pure narrative with no metric nearby to pair it with, or — the real reason to keep two slides — **a quote making a genuinely different point than the stats** (the stats prove cost savings, the quote is about ease of onboarding): that actually is two ideas, and merging it would blur the slide rather than sharpen it. "They're different content types" is not, on its own, that reason — a stats row and a quote are different *shapes* of evidence for one claim, which is exactly what an asymmetric split exists to hold.

### Comparison & relationships
- **Comparison v1 (side by side).** Two panels: neutral gray (dark text) vs **brand-purple gradient** (white text, Smartcat monogram top-right). The purple side reads as "ours"/preferred. Each = heading + bullets.
- **Comparison v2 (2×2 with flow).** Two stacked rows of the v1 pairing, with a downward chevron between the two brand panels to show progression.
- **Alignment matrix.** Two columns of numbered cards — **purple numbers = Smartcat (left), black numbers = client (right)** — with center labels between them and inward-pointing chevrons. "Smartcat" label top-left, client logo top-right.

### Time & structure
- **Timeline.** A horizontal center bar (alternating light/dark purple segments); milestones alternate above and below with dotted connectors; each = bold title + muted "(Day N)" + paragraph. Compose from token-colored bar + positioned text blocks. **For 4–6 plain milestones, a two-column ruled list (date │ event) usually beats the graphic** — it reads faster and it survives export to native Slides shapes. Keep the horizontal timeline for when the *shape* of the schedule is the point: overlaps, phases, or a long run of milestones.
- **Gantt.** A table with a Workstream/Description column and a numbered week or named month axis; process bars span columns — **solid purple = completed, light outline = future** — with a Completed/Future legend. Month variant adds dotted vertical gridlines.
- **Flow / graph.** When the flow IS the slide's whole content (e.g. "How it works" in N steps), build it as a row of **icon-bearing cards connected by arrows** — each node is a small card (icon + label, optional one-line caption), not a bare pill, so every step carries real visual weight instead of leaving the lower half of the slide empty. Reserve the bare brand-purple pill node for a *secondary*, compact appearance: a short process strip stacked above a supporting detail-card row that carries the real content (see "Asymmetric & stacked layouts" above) — there, the pills read as a compact index into the cards below rather than needing to fill the slide on their own. Node boxes/circular nodes connected by dotted arrows, optionally grouped under "Timeline point" columns divided by baseline rules, remain the fuller treatment for a flow that genuinely branches or needs milestones grouped under headers.

### Tabular
- **Table.** Bold column headers, thin row dividers, muted values. Highlight the key column with a gray fill (e.g. the label column of a pricing table); add a bold summary/total row below when relevant. Compose from token-styled table markup. **Tabular figures** (`font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums`) in every column of numbers, here and in any stat ladder — proportional digits leave a column of figures ragged. One table worth naming as a role of its own: the **reader-routing matrix** — "who you are / what you care about / what you'll learn", one row per audience. It turns a generic document into several personalised ones for the cost of a single slide, and belongs near the front of a read deck.

### Closing
- **Back cover.** The deck's last slide and the cover's bookend: the **cover gradient** again, a short sign-off at Display scale top-left ("Thank you!"), the cover's own metadata line repeated bottom-left in pink-40, and the Smartcat wordmark bottom-right. It is the only slide besides the cover that carries the wordmark, and the only other place the cover gradient is allowed. Every deck ends with one; it follows the closing slide below rather than replacing it — that one makes the ask, this one signs off. Built with `add_back_cover`.
- **Closing / thank-you.** Title top-left ("Questions? Thank you."), on a brand-gradient, dark, or gray background — mirror the cover's treatment. Optional contact info or next-step links, set as plain/link-styled text — **not `.btn`** (see "No interactive UI" in Design DNA). Build from `heading`. An **author or contact chip** — circle-crop photo + name + role + an email address as plain text — is the sanctioned way to make a sent deck feel like a person wrote it. Still no button, and still only one accent job on the slide.

## D. The decision procedure — turning content into a slide

**Pass 0 — the deck before the slides.** Four checks, cheap now and expensive later:

- **Read or presented?** Decide before designing anything. A deck that gets *sent* — a business case, a proposal, a QBR left behind — carries real paragraphs, roughly 100–250 words a slide, with sources and footnotes. That is the density this catalog assumes and Smartcat's usual case. A deck *presented live* runs nearer 25 words a slide and lets the speaker carry the rest. Thin the copy for a presented deck, not the layout: the same recipes, fewer words per panel.
- **The headline test.** Write every slide's headline in order and read only those. They should make the argument on their own. If they don't, no layout will save the deck — fix the headlines before designing a single slide. It is also the fastest way to find the slide that isn't pulling its weight (step 1 below).
- **Does the deck breathe?** Never three dense slides in a row without a quiet one — a statement, a divider, a single stat. Together with the light/dark banding in section B, that rhythm is what makes a long deck scannable.
- **Skeleton before skin.** Block out each slide as bands and column spans first — what sits where, what is left empty — and only then apply tokens, colour and imagery. A layout that doesn't work in grey doesn't work.

Run these in order for each slide:

1. **Does this content deserve to be its own slide?** Two checks, in this order.
   **First, is the slide next to it making the same point?** Two adjacent slides arguing one thing are one slide. The clearest case is proof: a row of customer stats and a customer quote are both evidence for one claim — typically some form of "customers get the results they came to Smartcat for" — so they belong together, quote on one side and the figures backing the same claim on the other, as an asymmetric split (see "Testimonial" in section C for both ways this comes up). Merging them this way IS one idea per slide, not an exception to it: the numbers are the quantitative form of the idea, the quote is the qualitative form, and neither is a second, competing claim. Ask this before anything else, because merging two slides changes what each of them needs to contain. (The same logic runs the other way when one slide carries two genuinely different arguments — that is two slides.)

   Two objections come up here, and both mistake this merge for an exception to one-idea-per-slide rather than a normal case of it. **"They're different content types, each already sized to fill their own slide"** is not evidence they need separate slides — sizing to fill a slide on its own is just the single-role recipe's default, not a requirement; the split panel's own sizing rules (a stat figure steps to H1 below ~260px, a quote sets as bold pull-quote prose with no gradient panel — see Design DNA "Surfaces" and the Testimonial recipe) exist specifically so the merged form doesn't fall below the same legibility floor the standalone forms use. **"It blurs one-idea-per-slide pacing into two beats"** gets the rule backwards — the test was never "is this one visual element," it's "is this one claim." Four different numbers in a stats row are already several beats read as one idea; a quote saying the same thing in a customer's own words is the same move, not a second beat. The test to actually run is **do the stats and the quote support the same underlying claim?** If yes, merge — that is one idea per slide, correctly applied. If the quote is making a genuinely different point (the stats prove cost savings, the quote praises onboarding), keep them apart — that is two ideas, not one split across two content types. Only once they share a claim does overflow become the practical question: if the combined content doesn't fit the split's two panels at their documented floor sizes, split it — but that's a rare constraint, not the default reason to keep two slides.
   **Then, is what remains thin?** Check it isn't so thin it would leave most of the canvas empty — a bare title + one short sentence, a single stat with nothing else, a pill chain with no supporting row. If it is, fold it into an adjacent slide under one shared idea instead of giving it a slide of its own — see Design DNA, "A slide needs enough content to earn the canvas."
2. **What is this slide's *job*?** Map the intent to a role in section C:
   - Open / divide a section → cover / section divider
   - Land one idea → one big statement
   - Explain in prose → heading + paragraph (with a short supporting list, if needed) / N columns
   - Enumerate parallel items, however short → N cards / icon cards / grouped tiles / agenda
   - Quantify or prove → numbers / charts / key metrics / testimonial
   - Contrast options → comparison v1 or v2 / alignment matrix
   - Show sequence or plan → icon-card flow chain / timeline / Gantt / flow-graph
   - Present rows of data → table
   - Close → thank-you

   Whatever role you picked, also ask: **does this slide's content name a specific product surface** — a UI view, a conversation, a before/after, a dashboard? This is orthogonal to the role above, not a role of its own, and it applies to every one of them, including a single step inside a flow chain. If yes, reserve an image slot as part of building this slide, right now — not a review pass afterward, and not conditional on already having the screenshot (Design DNA, "A slide about a product surface reserves room for it"). A flow-chain step naming a real UI moment may be significant enough to earn its own slide (an `add_image_banner`) rather than staying folded into a node caption. `check_missing_image_slots` (in `deck_pptx.py`) is a mechanical backstop for this exact question — written for the case where the rule is known and simply never gets asked while planning — but it is a backstop, not a substitute for asking it here.
3. **How much content, how many items — and does it want an even shape or an uneven one?** Item count narrows the options; it doesn't hand you a single answer:
   - 1 item → full-width or centered.
   - 2–4 *genuinely parallel* items (same weight, same structure) → an even `.grid` row of panels/columns — N cards, N stats. This is still the right call whenever the items really are peers.
   - One headline element plus a larger block of supporting detail (a framing sentence next to a card grid; one highlighted stat next to a comparison table) → an **asymmetric split** at an uneven ratio instead of forcing an even division.
   - A summary plus the detail behind it (a process chain and what backs each step; a quote and its attribution) → **stack** two components vertically in one slide rather than only side by side.
   - Many shallow items, genuinely parallel → **cards**, not a list — a plain bullet list never carries a whole slide once there are 3+ real peers (see Text-forward and Enumerated content in section C). Paired items → side-by-side panels.
   Respect the "whitespace is deliberate" invariant — if it's getting cramped, split into two slides. Respect the opposite too — "a slide needs enough content to earn the canvas" (step 1) — if it's coming up empty, merge instead of stretching one thin idea across a whole slide.

   **None of this makes the even card row or a plain heading+paragraph a worse choice** — for content that's actually parallel or actually a single idea, they're the correct, most-used answer in this catalog, not a fallback. The failure mode isn't using them; it's reaching for them by reflex on every slide without checking whether *this* slide's content is actually asymmetric or layered. Run this step as a real choice among the options above each time, not a formality on the way to the default.
4. **Where does this slide sit in the deck's rhythm?** Pick theme + background per section B — alternate light/dark in bands, reserve gradients for dividers and the open/close.
5. **Compose it** from tokens + atomic components (section A + the recipe). Reshape components as needed; keep every color/size/space/radius a token.
6. **Sanity-check against the DNA (A):** right title placement (cover = bottom-left, else top-left); period-style headline where it fits; semantic colors only for real positive/negative; brand gradient only on a hero moment; panels styled per light/dark; generous whitespace; **every card carries an icon or a numbered badge, except stat cards**; **every card's height matches its own content, with the row centered when it leaves extra vertical room** — never stretched or pinned-with-dead-space-below; **any missing image or logo asset is a labeled placeholder, not an omission**; **a flow that is the whole slide's content is icon-cards-with-arrows, not bare pills**; **for every gap between adjacent elements, check whether both sides have a surface — gutter (8px) if both do, slide padding (48px) if either is bare**; **no `.btn` anywhere — any call to action or link is plain/link-styled text.** Then the deck-wide ones: **no chrome — no page numbers, running heads, per-slide logos or rails**; **exactly one accent job, and no label above any title**; **every figure rounded and every data slide credited with its source**; **a reserved image slot wherever the slide names a product surface — and nowhere else — with the shot list reported on handoff**; **no device used on this slide that isn't used elsewhere in the deck** — used once it is decoration, used throughout it is a system, and a recurring reshape graduates into a documented component variant (see the top of this doc).

When two roles both fit the content equally well, choose the one that needs the least visual machinery — a clean heading + paragraph beats a forced diagram. This is a tie-breaker between options that already fit, not a reason to skip step 3's check of whether an asymmetric or stacked shape fits *better* than the even default.

## E. Extending the brain

This doc is meant to grow as real example slides are provided. When given a new example (or a correction):

1. **Inspect it visually** (render to an image and look — don't infer from text).
2. **Extract, in this order:**
   - **Background & theme** — which of section B, light or dark.
   - **Layout skeleton** — title position, how the canvas is divided (columns/rows/panels), what's left empty.
   - **Atomic pieces used** — which atomic/existing components, and how they were *reshaped* (e.g. horizontalized card, mixed sub-components).
   - **Tokens** — the specific color/type/spacing/radius roles in play, especially any semantic or brand-gradient use.
   - **Anything new** — a pattern not already in section C.
3. **Fold it in:** refine an existing recipe in C, or add a new role; adjust A or B only if the example reveals a genuine invariant. Keep recipes short and composition-based. Log it below.

Do **not** move deck reasoning into `CLAUDE.md` — that file holds only the hard, binding rules; the how-to-think stays here.

- Reserved product-UI image slots (2026-09-21) — decks carried no imagery at all; a set of reference decks (`Deck refs/Image placement`) was reviewed for where product shots actually sit. Added a **reserved image slot**: a deliberate layout reservation for a screenshot inserted by hand later, kept strictly separate from the existing missing-asset placeholder (different purpose, different drawing — the slot is brand-purple and states its exact pixel size and ratio, because someone has to produce an image of that shape). Three shapes, all observed in the references: a side panel in a split (the default, 3:2), a full-width banner, and a card-row cell. Slots go only where a slide names a specific product surface, never on every slide. `image_slots(prs)` lists them for the handoff shot list, which is now a required step: a dashed box reaching a customer is worse than no image.
