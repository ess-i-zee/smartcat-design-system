Marketing transcreation is how global brands make campaigns land in new markets without losing the idea that made them work at home. Translate a campaign literally and it falls flat, or backfires; rebuild it from scratch in every market and costs explode. Transcreation is the middle path. Below are nine documented examples — seven successes and two cautionary tales — followed by the best practices that separate them.

Key takeaways

  • Marketing transcreation adapts the idea, not the words: taglines, jingles, imagery, and even product positioning are recreated so the campaign triggers the same response in a new market.
  • The most successful transcreations — Share a Coke in China, De Beers’ “a diamond is forever,” McDonald’s “i’m lovin’ it” — changed the message significantly to keep the effect identical.
  • The famous failures — KFC’s “eat your fingers off,” Pampers’ stork in Japan — are what happens when copy or imagery crosses borders unexamined.
  • Transcreation is priced and briefed like creative work: per concept or per hour, not per word, with in-market copywriters rather than translators alone.

Marketing transcreation in brief

What is the purpose of transcreation?

To carry a message’s emotional impact and persuasive intent into a new market, not just its words — the difference that matters most for taglines, campaigns, and brand copy.

What’s the difference between transcreation and translation?

Translation reproduces meaning accurately, word for word; transcreation reproduces impact, with license to change the words, images, and ideas entirely.

Isn’t transcreation just localization?

No — localization adapts formats, units, and references while staying close to the original wording. Transcreation goes further and may replace the wording, or even the concept, when emotional impact matters more than accuracy.

What is the transcreation process?

Creative brief, source analysis, concept adaptation, native copywriting with back-translations, in-market review, and validation — closer to a copywriting engagement than a translation job.

Coca-Cola: “Share a Coke” name swaps

Coca-Cola’s “Share a Coke” campaign printed popular first names on bottles, starting in Australia in 2011 before rolling out worldwide. In China, printing personal names would have missed the mark — addressing someone by their given name can feel overly familiar, and personal names on consumer goods carried different connotations. So the campaign used relationship terms and affectionate nicknames like “classmate” and “close friend” instead. For Lunar New Year in Malaysia, the mechanic shifted again, with bottles carrying New Year’s wishes instead of names. The device — find the bottle that speaks to someone you care about — stayed identical throughout; only the words changed.

McDonald’s: “i’m lovin’ it” in China

McDonald’s launched “i’m lovin’ it” as its first single global campaign in 2003 — but “global” didn’t mean identical everywhere. In China, the word “love” is weighty and rarely declared casually in public, so a literal rendering would have felt awkward rather than upbeat. The Chinese version, 我就喜欢 — roughly “I just like it” — swapped the verb but kept the casual, slightly defiant youth attitude the campaign was built on. Dozens of other markets received their own adaptations tuned the same way: same brand feeling, locally natural phrasing rather than a word-for-word match.

De Beers: “A diamond is forever” in China

De Beers’ “a diamond is forever” — coined in 1947 and often called the greatest tagline of the 20th century — entered China as 钻石恒久远，一颗永流传: “a diamond is eternal, one passes down forever.” The transcreation reads like a classical poetic couplet, borrowing the cadence of Chinese literary tradition, and tied diamonds to permanence and inheritance in a market with no existing diamond engagement-ring custom. That line didn’t just translate a slogan — it helped build the tradition itself, and China grew into one of the world’s largest diamond jewelry markets. Few campaigns show more clearly how transcreation can create a category, not just sell into one.

Haribo: a jingle rebuilt in every market

Haribo’s German jingle — “Haribo macht Kinder froh, und Erwachsene ebenso” — rhymes, scans, and sticks in a way a literal translation never could. Translating it would have preserved the meaning (“Haribo makes children happy, and adults as well”) while killing the music entirely. Instead, each market got the jingle recreated from scratch: English speakers know it as “Kids and grown-ups love it so, the happy world of Haribo.” The rhyme, meter, and melody survive; the literal wording does not. It’s one of the cleanest illustrations of a transcreator’s brief — the deliverable is the earworm, not the sentence.

Intel Brazil: “Sponsors of Tomorrow” becomes “In love with the future”

When Intel took its “Sponsors of Tomorrow” campaign to Brazil, testing revealed a problem: in Brazilian Portuguese, the line implied Intel promises the future but doesn’t deliver it yet — “tomorrow” read as delay, not vision. The transcreated line, “Apaixonados pelo futuro” (“In love with the future”), kept the forward-looking brand idea while dodging the unintended reading, and matched the emotional register Brazilian audiences respond to. The lesson generalizes well beyond Intel: even a “safe,” abstract, non-idiomatic tagline can carry connotations that only in-market testing and a native copywriter will catch.

Red Bull: repositioned for China

Red Bull’s China strategy shows transcreation extending beyond copy into the product and positioning themselves. For the Chinese market, Red Bull has been sold in a distinctive gold can with red-and-gold styling — colors associated with prosperity and good fortune — and in a less-carbonated formulation suited to local taste preferences, positioned closer to a functional tonic than a Western party drink. The brand idea, energy and vitality, stayed constant, but nearly every executional element around it changed. It’s brand transcreation at its most complete: not a line rewritten, but a product reconceived for the market it’s entering.

“Got Milk?” for US Hispanic audiences

The California Milk Processor Board’s “Got Milk?” is beloved in English, but a literal Spanish rendering — “¿Tienes leche?” — can read as “Are you lactating?” Worse, the campaign’s central joke, running out of milk at an inconvenient moment, didn’t resonate with Hispanic family audiences, for whom running out of milk isn’t funny so much as a real household problem. The board’s Hispanic-market campaigns were rebuilt from the concept up: family, cooking, and abundance replaced the original punchline entirely. It’s a double lesson — check the literal reading first, then check whether the joke itself even survives the trip.

Cautionary tale: KFC’s “finger-lickin’ good” in China

When KFC opened in Beijing in the late 1980s, its famous slogan “finger-lickin’ good” was rendered into Chinese in a way that read as “eat your fingers off.” The brand recovered, but the phrase remains the industry’s standard cautionary tale decades later. Strictly, this was a translation failure rather than a transcreation one — exactly the kind of error transcreation exists to prevent. Idioms are the highest-risk content category in any market entry: they should never cross a border without a native, in-market copywriter and a back-translation check before anything ships.

Cautionary tale: Pampers’ stork in Japan

When Procter & Gamble launched Pampers in Japan, packaging and ads used the Western image of a stork delivering a baby. Japanese parents were reportedly confused: the stork story isn’t part of Japanese folklore, where tradition instead has babies arriving inside giant peaches, the Momotaro tale. The imagery didn’t offend anyone — it simply carried no meaning, and the ads lost their intended warmth as a result. The lesson extends transcreation past words entirely: visual metaphors are just as culture-bound as language, and imagery needs the same in-market review that copy gets.

Advertising transcreation vs marketing translation

Advertising transcreation and marketing translation are complementary, not competing disciplines. The 40-second version: transcreation is for content whose job is emotional impact; translation, with human review, is for content whose job is accuracy. Most global marketing operations run both, routing each asset to the right process rather than forcing everything through one.

Advertising transcreation Marketing translation
Best for Taglines, slogans, campaign concepts, jingles, OOH and video ads, brand names Product pages, brochures, email flows, blog content, case studies, docs
Goal Same emotional impact, wording free to change Accurate meaning, brand voice preserved
Who does it In-market copywriter/transcreator Professional translator + reviewer
Source material Creative brief and brand strategy Source text, TM, glossary
Deliverable Several options + back-translation + rationale One translated text
Pricing model Per concept, per hour, or per project Per word
Volume Low (dozens of words) High (thousands of words)

The practical rule: if a literal, accurate translation of the asset would still do its job, it’s translation work. If accuracy alone would leave it flat, brief it as transcreation.

Marketing transcreation best practices

Six practices separate the De Beers outcomes from the KFC ones.

1

Start from a creative brief, not the source text

Give transcreators the same brief your original creative team had: campaign objective, audience insight, tone, mandatories, and what the line must make people feel. A transcreator working only from finished copy can merely rewrite it; one working from the brief can recreate it.

2

Use in-market copywriters, not just translators

Transcreation needs someone who lives in the target culture and writes persuasively in it. Bilingual fluency isn’t sufficient — Intel’s Brazil problem was invisible to anyone who wasn’t natively attuned to how “tomorrow” lands in Brazilian Portuguese. Source native, in-market creative talent for every launch market.

3

Require back-translations and rationale for sign-off

Because reviewers at HQ can’t read every target language, require each option come with a back-translation (literal English rendering) and a short rationale explaining what was changed and why. This is how brand teams approve work they can’t read — and how “eat your fingers off” gets caught before launch.

4

Test before you launch

Concept-test transcreated lines with target-market audiences, even informally. Intel caught its Brazil problem in testing; KFC’s error shipped. A few days of in-market validation is the cheapest insurance in global marketing.

5

Protect brand voice with glossaries and translation memory

Transcreation grants creative license, but product names, claims, and legal phrases must stay fixed. Maintain glossaries (never-translate and must-translate terms) and translation memory so approved brand language stays consistent across every campaign and channel — especially when transcreated ads and translated landing pages ship together.

6

Budget per concept, not per word

A transcreated tagline may be four words that took two days of exploration, options, and testing. Pricing it per word guarantees you’ll get translation, not transcreation. Budget per concept, per hour, or per project, and scope in rounds of options like any other creative engagement.

Frequently asked questions

What is marketing transcreation?

Marketing transcreation is the creative adaptation of marketing content — taglines, campaigns, ads, brand messaging — for a new market, preserving the original’s intent and emotional impact rather than its literal wording. A transcreator works from the creative brief and may change words, imagery, and even the concept so the campaign triggers the same response locally.

What are examples of successful transcreation campaigns?

The classic references: De Beers’ “a diamond is forever” became a poetic Chinese couplet that helped create the country’s diamond engagement ring tradition; McDonald’s “i’m lovin’ it” became “I just like it” in China, where “love” is too weighty for casual use; and Coca-Cola’s “Share a Coke” swapped first names for nicknames like “close friend” in China. Haribo’s per-market jingle and Intel’s Brazil tagline round out the list above.

How much does marketing transcreation cost?

Transcreation is priced like creative work: per concept, per hour, or per project — not per word, since the deliverable is a small amount of copy backed by significant exploration, options, back-translations, and rationale. Rates vary widely by market and provider; reserve per-word pricing for marketing translation instead.

What’s the difference between transcreation and creative translation?

They overlap, and some providers use the terms interchangeably. In practice, creative translation stays anchored to the source text while allowing stylistic freedom; transcreation starts from the brief and may abandon the source wording entirely — new metaphors, new references, sometimes a new concept. If the deliverable includes multiple options with rationale, you’re buying transcreation.
